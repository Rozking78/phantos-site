4Groups & masters

A group is a named set of fixtures. It is also, when you want it to be, a master that rides above everything else in the desk.

Making and using groups

How do I make a group?
  1. Select the fixtures you want.
  2. Type record group 4 — or tap Record, then a group tile.
  3. label group 4 Backlight gives it a name.
  • group 4 selects its members. group next / group prev walk the pool.
  • group 4 @ 60 sets its members to 60%.
  • group 4 + adds it to the current selection instead of replacing it.
  • merge group 4 adds the current selection to the group; update group 4 replaces its members with the current selection.

The group fader is the master

The group fader lives outside the programmer

A group fader tile in the workspace is the group's master — the same level as the group edit sheet's Level fader. It is not programmer content: Clear never moves it, so a spot held on a performer survives Clear and Release All. Moving the level of a group that has no master mode yet arms it inhibitive and the console says so on the status line — and it does that from every level surface: the fader tile, the group sheet's Level fader, the group tile's Masters menu and group 4 level 50 all behave the same way. Its LCD glows while the master is actually in force: an inhibitive master below full, or an additive master above zero. Its LCD also names the mode next to the group number — INHIB, ADD, MASTER OFF or PROG — so a fader that is doing nothing tells you why.

Putting a master back. Because Clear cannot touch it, a master needs an explicit release, and the fader carries one: long-press the LCD and press Reset to neutral (or type fader 2 1 reset). Neutral is the position at which the master stops changing the output — full for an inhibitive group or an executor master, the bottom of the throw for an additive group. The same row tells you when a fader is not a master, and which key does clear it, so you never have to guess which of your faders answers Clear.

Prog Mode (optional, per tile — long-press the tile, MODE row): the fader becomes a programmer control instead. Grabbing it recalls the group and drives member intensity — exactly group N @ L written as a fader — so Clear zeroes what it reads and typed landings fade when the fade is armed.

Reach the master by holding a group tile — or right-clicking it, or arming Edit and tapping it; all three open the same sheet in one step: Mode, Level (0–255) and Priority live on that sheet, as a row of mode capsules and two faders you can grab with a glove on.

Master modes

ModeWhat it does to its members' intensity
NoneThe master is off. The level does nothing and the group is just a selection shortcut. A group fader tile says MASTER OFF on its LCD so you can see it from across the stage — and moving any level surface arms Inhibitive.
InhibitiveA sub-master. Scales member intensity by the level — at half, the group can never exceed half of whatever the rest of the desk asks for. At 0 it holds them out.
AdditivePins member intensity to the level. It does not add — it overwrites, whatever the programmer or playback asked for.

Masters touch intensity only, and only for the fixtures in the group. They are applied at the very end of every frame, to both the programmer and the playback layer, so they are the last thing between your desk and the rig. Level runs 0–255, where 255 means no effect.

Not built yet

Assertion is refused, not offered: its tab on the mode row is drawn inert with the reason on it, and group 4 mode assertion is turned away on the command line with the same sentence. It needs its own priority source and arbitration between overlapping masters, and neither is built. Its neighbour Priority is stored but not yet consulted either. Use additive if you need a group to hold a value against the rest of the desk.

When a fixture is in two master groups

There is no arbitration between masters — they apply in group-number order, lowest first, and each one acts on what the previous one left:

  • Two inhibitive groups multiply — half of a half is a quarter.
  • An additive group wipes any scaling a lower-numbered group applied, because it pins.
  • An inhibitive group scales a pin set by a lower-numbered group.

In short: the highest-numbered master has the last word. If a rig is behaving oddly, look for a high-numbered master group before you look anywhere else.

The move this is for

How do I keep a spot on a performer through the end-of-song blackout?
  1. Make a group containing the followspot.
  2. Open its properties and set its mode to Additive.
  3. Set its Level to where you want the performer lit.
  4. At the button: Clear (twice) and Release All.
  5. The programmer and every executor let go. The additive master keeps the spot on the performer through the blackout.

An additive group still holding light after a full clear is correct — that is the feature, not a stuck value. A master is rig configuration, so neutralising it is a deliberate act: change its Level or its mode from the group's own surfaces. No clear, release or master release will do it for you.

The Grid — where the group physically hangs

A group's Grid is graph paper. Drag its members into the shape they hang in — a wall, an arch, a truss line — and the console stops guessing. Two things then read the layout instead of the fixture numbers: the Align fan on its X and Y axes, and effect spread. Fan a pan across a wall and it runs left to right across the real wall.

The layout belongs to the group. It travels in the show file, it follows the group when you reorder the pool, and it follows the fixtures through a renumber.

  • Recall a group to load its saved layout. An unsaved group follows its members’ canvas positions until you edit or save the grid. Fixtures without canvas placement stay unplaced; several fixtures at the same centre default are not a layout. Reset remains a creative square arrangement.
  • Fit shows the occupied extent, including layouts offset from zero. It re-fits when the layout or window size changes; Zoom 1:1 returns to the normal cell size. Cells keep their touch size, so very dense layouts still scroll. Both controls work on scratch paper too.
  • From Output deliberately replaces the current layout with current canvas positions. Use it after moving a rig whose grid was already saved. grid fromoutput does the same. With no distinct canvas geometry it refuses and leaves your layout alone.
  • Tap a fixture, then tap a cell to place it. For a staggered rig, the PLACE INTO row appears the moment something is selected: choose Whole cell, Right half, Bottom half or Corner, and the next cell you tap takes the fixture in that slot. Lasso to select several; hold an empty cell to add it to a batch (⇧-click does the same with a mouse), and Fill drops the unplaced ones into the cells you selected.
  • Hold a placed fixture — or right-click it — to remove it from the grid, or to remove the whole selection at once.
  • Update Grid saves. Reset reflows everything into a square by ascending fixture number.
  • The grid selection is the programmer selection, and it carries the order you can see — reading order across the layout, not fixture number. Save that as a group and it fans in that order.
  • No group recalled? The Grid opens on scratch paper holding your selection — or the whole patched rig when nothing is selected. Drag it into shape and press Save as Group: the fixtures you can see become the group, in the order they read across the paper, and the arrangement is saved onto the group with them. With nothing to save the console says so rather than recording an empty group.
  • Tap a scratch cell to select the fixture in it, the same as on a group's grid — tap again to drop it. The selection fans in the order the paper reads, so a column you dragged fans as a column. Selecting narrows what you send to the programmer; it does not narrow the paper.
  • The scratch arrangement stays where you put it. Selecting, deselecting or editing the patch no longer re-flows the paper: a fixture that leaves the patch leaves the paper, a fixture that arrives takes a free cell, and everything you dragged stays dragged. It lives for as long as the Grid is open — Save as Group is what makes it permanent, and the layout then travels in the show file on that group.

The Grid's selection is the programmer's selection, both ways. Select on the paper and the console follows; select on a pad, a tile or the command line with the Grid open and the paper follows back. A Clear anywhere drops the highlight everywhere. You will never lose a selection to a stale one the Grid was still holding.

Auto Array — lay a rig out over the canvas

Auto Array in the Grid header positions media fixtures and cameras across the canvas, or across one patched output, in a single move. It works on the recalled group; with no group recalled it takes your selection, and failing that the whole rig — you do not have to build a group first.

  • Grid best-fits everything into rows and columns that match the target's shape. Liner spreads them evenly around its perimeter.
  • Pick the target: the canvas, or any patched Phantos Output. An output target is the region that output is looking at — its position and scale on the canvas — so the array lands on the part of the wall that output actually shows, not on the canvas at large.
  • Zoom optionally scales each fixture's content to fit its cell — by width, by height, or fill.
  • The popover tells you how many fixtures it is about to position and draws them before you commit. One Undo reverts the whole array.
  • Auto Array writes canvas position and optional zoom. Unsaved geometry follows the new positions. Saved or hand-edited grid layouts remain yours; press From Output to replace one deliberately. Update Grid saves the paper; it does not read output positions.
  • When it is done it reports what it wrote beside the button — not how many fixtures it was offered. A fixture whose profile is not loaded is skipped, and the count says "3 of 5" rather than pretending all five moved.
  • On the command line: grid auto (grid + fill), or grid auto liner none and friends.

Straighten — when the grid comes out sideways

A layout that arrived rotated or mirrored is one tap from straight. Straighten in the Grid header opens the whole set as a pop-out list, and each one has a command-line twin:

Do thisCommand line
Rotate the layout 90° clockwise / anticlockwise / 180°grid rotate cw · grid rotate ccw · grid rotate 180
Mirror it left-to-right or top-to-bottomgrid mirror h · grid mirror v
Swap rows and columnsgrid transpose
Pull half-cell placements back onto whole cellsgrid snap
Close up empty rows and columnsgrid compact
Start again: one row, left to rightgrid reseed ltr
Start again: a squaregrid reseed square

Every one of these changes the layout only — no fixture moves, no value is written.

The grid verbs refuse rather than pretend. grid save needs a recalled group to save the layout onto and says so when there is none — the layout lives on the group, so with no group there is nowhere to put it. grid place refuses a fixture number that is not patched instead of storing a cell no fixture will ever stand on. grid load refuses a group that does not exist rather than leaving the console holding one. And align axis x (or y/xy) tells you when there is no grid to fan along: it still sets the axis, and warns that the fan will run in selection order until the selection has a layout.

Align follows the grid of the group you are holding

Bitmap / pixel mapper: patch a Bitmap fixture, recall the target group and arrange its Grid. Press Bind Bitmap, choose that fixture, then choose its NDI source. Select the Bitmap in the programmer and raise Color Xfade to map RGB, or Intensity Xfade to map picture brightness. Both start at zero. Position X/Y, Zoom and H/V Scale move and scale the picture; 128 is neutral. These seven ordinary attributes can be recorded in presets and cues. The Grid cells show the final output returned by Babel. Remove the binding or set both crossfades to zero to return to the underlying look; losing the video returns to that look within one second. Live Op keeps control. Overlapping bitmap groups blend in ascending group-number order. The video proxy is at most 64 pixels on its longest edge and updates at up to 30 Hz.

From the command line use grid bitmap <fixture|none> [group] and the existing ndi <fixture> <source name>. NDI reception runs on the Command that owns the Bitmap. Keep that Command online while playing mapped cues.

The Align window's X and Y axes fan along the grid. They light up when the selection actually has a layout, and stay dim when it does not — the console will not pretend to fan along geometry it does not have. # is the honest axis with no grid: it fans in selection order.

You do not have to recall a group to get its grid. Select a group's fixtures any way you like — the tiles, a command-line range, a pad — and if that selection is a group, Align picks up that group's layout. Select something that is not a group and the X/Y axes go dim rather than fanning along a stale layout.

Fan per group, the switch between the mode pads and the axis pads, decides what a fan spreads across. On: a selection holding several groups fans each group over its own range. Off: one fan runs across the whole selection.

An armed fan says so, and a fan never spreads a discrete value

A fan is a mode, not a window. Arm one and it keeps spreading every value you set long after you have closed the Align window and moved on — which is how a fan turns into a mystery: a spread you did not ask for that reads on stage as extra beams.

So the console says it. Whenever a fan mode is armed and more than one fixture is selected, an amber FAN chip appears on the Programmer header and beside the command line, naming the mode, the axis and how many fixtures the spread lands on — FAN <> · X — 6 fixtures. The Align bezel button wears the same thing on its face and goes green. Tap ○ on the Align strip in the Programmer to disarm, and every chip clears at once.

A fan spreads levels, positions and colours. It never spreads a discrete value. A dropdown, a button row, a toggle, a latch and a one-shot button are lists of named states — Blend Mode, Gobo, Focus Mode, Freeze, Cut — not scales, so there is nothing between two of their options to spread across. Pick one with a fan armed and it is a destination: every selected fixture lands on the value you picked, exactly as it would with the fan off. That includes the one-shot buttons — a Take or a One Push AF fires on every selected fixture, and an armed fan can never swallow it.

So every row stays live under a fan. You do not have to disarm anything to change a gobo, flip a freeze or hit a cut; the chip is there to tell you the faders are still spreading, which is the thing you can otherwise forget.

Two things deliberately do not count as a fan: Equal, which lands every fixture on one value rather than spreading them, and a single-fixture selection, which has nothing to spread across. Both leave the chip hidden.
