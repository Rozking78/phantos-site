Operator Manual / How the console thinks

1How the console thinks

Command is a nodal console. You do not drive lamps, cameras and switchers with three different tools — you drive fixtures, and a fixture is anything with a profile: a moving head, a media layer, a PTZ camera, an ATEM, a display. One selection language, one programmer, one record button, whatever the fixture happens to be.

The chain, front to back

Every value you put on stage travels the same road. Knowing the road tells you where to look when something is not doing what you asked.

StageWhat lives there
SelectionThe fixtures your next command will touch. Local to you — nobody else's selection moves when yours does.
ProgrammerWhat you are building right now, live on stage, on top of everything else. It is yours; another operator's programmer is a separate layer.
PresetsLooks you saved out of the programmer, by type — intensity, position, colour, gobo, beam and the rest.
Executors & cuesPlayback. Cue lists that run under the programmer and can be released without touching what you are holding.
Group mastersRig configuration that scales or pins whole groups at the very end. Not programmer content — see Groups & masters.
Grand masterThe last word on intensity, bottom-right of the screen.
The wireCommand sends resolved values to Babel. Babel merges every console in the session and talks to the actual gear. Command never speaks a device protocol itself.

The one habit that makes the desk make sense

Select, then act. Almost every command works on the current selection, so if a command did nothing, the first question is always what is selected? — not is the command broken?

With nothing selected, a value command does nothing at all. full, at 50, color red, a nudge, a panel fader, the deck's full key — every one of them needs a selection to aim at, and without one the console touches no fixture and says “no selection” on the status line. An empty selection is never read as everything. When you really do mean the whole rig, say so — 1 thru <last>, a group, or “all fixtures” out loud — and the console selects it first.

Three ways to give the same order

Every function has one implementation and several doors. Whichever you use, the result is identical and the status line at the bottom-right tells you what happened.

  • The command line — bottom of the screen. Fastest for anything with numbers in it.
  • Buttons — the bezel rows around the workspace, the tiles inside it, a Stream Deck, a foot pedal, a gamepad.
  • Your voice — the microphone beside the command line, or a comms channel. Spoken asks go to the AI lane, which proposes; nothing runs until you confirm.
How do I get a light on, from nothing?
  1. Click the command line at the bottom of the screen.
  2. Type 1 thru 8 @ full and press Return.
  3. Fixtures 1 to 8 are now in your programmer at 100%.
  4. Press Clear twice to give them back.

Reading the status line

The line at the right-hand end of the command bar is the console answering you. It says what it just did — “Cleared values — selection held; press again to deselect”, “Master Clear — all consoles”, “Cue Lists — Exec 3”. When a command seems to have been ignored, read it before you do anything else.

Getting back here

Click ? beside the command line to open this manual at the command reference. Hold ? down for the quick pop-up verb list instead — same verbs, no page change. You can also put Manual on a bezel button or a workspace tile, or open it on a second display from Settings ▸ Screen.

The Mac menu bar carries Help and nothing else — this manual, the GPW timeline section and the command reference. That is deliberate: the console is the menu. Nothing on this desk lives only up there, so a touchscreen with no mouse and no menu bar reaches every function. The three Help entries open the very page the ? opens.
